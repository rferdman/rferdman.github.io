User Guide
Blackbody Radiation Interactive Explorer

This tool lets you explore the three key properties of blackbody radiation: the Planck spectrum, Wien's displacement law, and the Stefan–Boltzmann law. Use the tabs at the top to switch between sections.

A blackbody is an idealised object that absorbs all radiation incident on it and re-emits according to its temperature alone. Stars are well approximated by blackbodies — their colour and luminosity are almost entirely determined by their surface temperature T.

Planck Spectrum. The Planck function Bλ(λ,T) = (2hc²/λ⁵)/(ehc/λkT−1) gives the spectral intensity emitted per unit wavelength. The curve's shape, peak position, and overall height all depend on T.

Controls
  • Temperature slider — drag to vary T from 1,000 to 50,000 K (log scale)
  • Star presets — click any pill to load that star's surface temperature
  • Pin curve — save the current curve and overlay a second for comparison (up to 3)
  • Show λ_max marker — toggle the dashed peak-wavelength line
Reading the plot
The y-axis is normalised to the current curve's peak = 1. Overlay curves are each normalised to their own peak, so you can compare shapes directly. The coloured strip at the bottom marks the visible spectrum (380–750 nm).

Wien's Displacement Law. The peak wavelength λmax = b/T, where b = 2.898×10−3 m·K. As temperature doubles, λmax halves — objects appear bluer as they get hotter.

The graph
The main panel plots log λmax vs log T. Because λmax ∝ T−1, this is a straight line of slope −1 on a log–log plot. Horizontal coloured bands show which part of the EM spectrum the peak falls in. The yellow cursor follows the current temperature.
Star dots
Named stars appear as coloured dots on the curve. The Sun (5,778 K) peaks in the green (≈502 nm), but we perceive it as white/yellow because all visible wavelengths are strongly emitted.

Stefan–Boltzmann Law. Total power radiated per unit surface area F = σT⁴. The graph plots log(F/F☉) vs T, showing how surface flux scales as T⁴ regardless of a star's size.

The graph
A log–log plot of F/F☉ vs T. The T⁴ relationship appears as a straight line of slope 4. Click any star in the sidebar to highlight it.
Doubling T
Double the temperature → luminosity increases by 2⁴ = 16×. This steep T⁴ dependence explains why blue supergiants are vastly more luminous than red dwarfs of similar size.
Verifying Wien's law
Switch to the Wien tab, note λmax at the Sun's temperature (5,778 K). Now halve T to 2,889 K — the peak wavelength should double. Try it.
Verifying Stefan–Boltzmann
In the Stefan–Boltzmann tab, increase T by 10% from the Sun (to 6,356 K). The luminosity increases by 1.1⁴ ≈ 1.46 — a 46% boost from a 10% temperature rise!
Comparing spectra
In the Spectrum tab, load Betelgeuse (3,500 K) and pin its curve. Then load the Sun (5,778 K). Notice the bluer peak, the larger amplitude, and how much more emission falls in the visible range.
