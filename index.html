About

Who I am

I am an Associate Professor of Astrophysics at the University of East Anglia, where I lead a research group focused on pulsar observations and their profound implications for our understanding of the universe.

Pulsars — the collapsed remnants of massive stars — are among the most extreme objects in the cosmos. Their extraordinary rotational stability makes them natural laboratories for probing physics impossible to replicate on Earth: from the behaviour of matter at nuclear densities to the curvature of spacetime itself.

My work spans the detection and timing of radio pulsars, the study of binary systems to probe stellar evolution, tests of fundamental theories of gravity, and the use of pulsar timing arrays as detectors for the nanohertz gravitational-wave background.

Increasingly, I am excited by the potential of artificial intelligence and machine learning to transform how we process and interpret the vast, complex datasets that modern radio astronomy produces — opening new windows on the universe that would otherwise remain shut.

Research

What I work on

Pulsar Observations & Timing

I observe and time radio pulsars using world-leading facilities including the UK's Lovell Telescope at Jodrell Bank Observatory, as well as Europe and the world's largest radio telescopes. Precise pulsar timing over years to decades reveals subtle changes in their rotation and orbital properties, encoding the physics of neutron-star interiors and magnetic fields, as well as their environments.

Binary & Massive Star Evolution

Many pulsars reside in binary systems, making them exceptional probes of stellar evolution. Pulse arrival times allow us to determine orbital properties, giving us access to the masses of the neutron stars within the observed systems. In doing so, I reconstruct the evolutionary histories of massive star pairs — including the conditions that precede both supernova explosions and neutron star formation, as well as their eventual mergers that can be detected by Earth-based gravitational-wave detectors.

Gravitational Waves & Theories of Gravity

Millisecond pulsars distributed across the sky form a galactic-scale detector — a Pulsar Timing Array — sensitive to nanohertz-frequency gravitational waves from supermassive black hole binaries and potentially, other cosmological sources. I also use binary pulsars to test the predictions of general relativity and alternative theories of gravity.

AI & Machine Learning in Radio Astronomy

The next generation of radio telescopes — including the Square Kilometre Array Observatory — will produce data volumes far beyond current sustainability. I aim to develop and apply machine learning techniques for automated pulsar detection, radio frequency interference mitigation, anomaly detection in timing data, and from these datasets, the extraction of astrophysical parameters using complex, high-dimensional models.

Neutron Star Physics

My research interests also include the study of neutron stars as fundamental objects: their equation of state, superfluid interior dynamics, magnetic field evolution, and glitch behaviour all involve some of the most extreme physics in the Universe. Pulsars are a powerful diagnostic tool for probing these phenomena — under conditions that are not reproducible in any Earth-based laboratory.

Multi-messenger Astronomy

Merging neutron star binaries produce both gravitational-wave signals detectable by LIGO/Virgo/Kagra and electromagnetic radiation that may be observed across the spectrum. I am interested in how observations and analysis of double neutron star binaries can complement and inform ground-based gravitational-wave detection strategies, subsequent multi-messenger observations, and the physics these will probe — from the equation of state of dense nuclear matter to the evolution of the Universe itself.
